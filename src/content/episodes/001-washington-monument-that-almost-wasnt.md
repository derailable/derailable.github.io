## Summary

Most people picture one Washington Monument: the white obelisk rising over the National Mall. But the race to honor George Washington produced three very different monuments, each with its own claim to history.

Baltimore began first, laying its cornerstone in 1815. Its monument became a classical column topped by a statue of Washington at the moment he resigned his military commission, an act that helped establish the peaceful transfer of power. In 1827, residents of Boonsboro marched up South Mountain and used stone gathered on site to build the first completed Washington Monument. Then there was Washington, D.C., whose enormous obelisk stalled for more than two decades. During the Civil War, its unfinished grounds were used as a cattle pen. Construction eventually resumed, and a metal so costly it was treated like silver capped what was then the tallest building in the world.

Along the way, a marble block sent by Pope Pius IX vanished, local residents hauled stones up a mountain, and three monuments arrived at three distinct kinds of first: **Baltimore started first. Boonsboro finished first. D.C. built the tallest.**

## Interesting Discoveries

- During the Civil War, the unfinished Washington Monument grounds in D.C. were used as a cattle pen. An 1862 cartoon called it the “Beef Depot Monument.”
- A commemorative marble block donated by Pope Pius IX was stolen in 1854. It was reportedly thrown into the Potomac and has never been recovered.
- The D.C. monument’s 100-ounce aluminum tip cost $225 in 1884, when aluminum was priced like silver rather than treated as an everyday metal.
- Baltimore’s monument portrays Washington resigning as commander-in-chief, with his commission in his outstretched hand toward Annapolis.
- In 1827, residents of Boonsboro carried local stone up South Mountain and completed the first monument in the United States memorializing George Washington.
